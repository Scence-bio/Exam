Lecture One
General Introduction to Entomology
Definition & Scope

Entomology is the scientific discipline concerned with the comprehensive study of insects. This encompasses their morphology (external structure), anatomy (internal structure), physiology (functional processes), and taxonomy (classification), as well as their ecological relationships with the environment and other organisms.

📌
Key point: Insects belong to the Phylum Arthropoda, which alone accounts for approximately 87% of all described animal species on Earth. The remaining 13% includes Mollusca (5%), Chordata (2%), and all other phyla.
Taxonomic Classification (Taxonomy)

All life is organised into a ranked hierarchy. From broadest to most specific:

Taxonomic Ranks
  • Domain — broadest grouping
  • Kingdom — e.g., Animalia
  • Phylum — e.g., Arthropoda
  • Class — e.g., Insecta
  • Order — e.g., Diptera
  • Family — e.g., Culicidae
  • Genus — e.g., Aedes
  • Species — e.g., Aedes aegypti
Six Kingdoms of Life
  • Animalia — multicellular, motile
  • Plantae — autotrophic, multicellular
  • Fungi — heterotrophic, mostly multicellular
  • Protista — unicellular or colonial
  • Eubacteria — prokaryotic, single-celled
  • Archaebacteria — found in extreme conditions
What is an Arthropod?

Arthropods are invertebrates (no backbone) with a hard external skeleton and jointed limbs. The name derives from Greek: arthro = joint, pod = foot.

15 Defining Characteristics of Arthropods
  • Exoskeleton — made of chitin; protects, prevents evaporation, supports muscles; insoluble in water
  • Moulting (ecdysis) — periodic shedding and replacement of the exoskeleton to allow growth
  • Jointed appendages — limbs that may be modified into mouthparts, antennae, or reproductive organs; either branched (biramous) or unbranched (uniramous)
  • Segmented body — segments fused into functional units called tagmata (head, thorax, abdomen) by a process called tagmosis
  • Bilateral symmetry — body divisible into two mirror-image halves
  • Open circulatory system — dorsal heart; blood (haemolymph) flows freely in haemocoel
  • Ectothermic (cold-blooded) — body temperature regulated by the environment
  • Tracheal respiration — network of tubes; some use book lungs
  • Separate sexes — internal fertilisation in most species; most are oviparous (egg-laying); some parthenogenetic
  • Pheromone communication — chemical signals for mating, alarm, food-finding, migration
  • Metamorphosis — hemimetabolous (egg → nymph → adult) or holometabolous (egg → larva → pupa → adult)
  • Haemocoel — body cavity divided into dorsal, ventral, and visceral sinuses
  • Dorsal vessel — carries haemolymph through the dorsal sinus
  • Compound & simple eyes, antennae, ganglia — acute sensory organs
  • Malpighian tubules or green glands — excretory organs; complete digestive tract
Six Classes of Arthropods
Class 1

Onychophora

Example: Peripatus. Cylindrical, worm-like body; no distinct head; one pair of antennae; one pair of legs per segment ending in claws. Lives in moist soil and bark; feeds on decomposed organic matter. Breathes via tracheae.

Class 2

Chilopoda — Centipedes

Head + trunk of similar segments; one pair of legs per segment; first pair modified into venomous claws (used to subdue prey). Biting mandibles; single pair of antennae; live in moist habitats (no waterproof cuticle). Fast-moving carnivores; can bite humans, causing pain. Secretes chemical defenses.

Class 3

Diplopoda — Millipedes

Head + trunk; ≥30 pairs of legs; most trunk segments carry two pairs of legs (segments 2–4 carry one pair only). One pair of antennae; respiration by trachea; excretion by Malpighian tubules. Feeds on living and dead plant matter; occasionally predacious.

Class 4

Arachnida — Spiders, Scorpions, Ticks, Mites

Two tagmata: cephalothorax (head + thorax fused, carries sensory organs, mouthparts, legs, stomach) and abdomen (heart, lungs, gut, reproductive organs). Six pairs of appendages: chelicerae (prey-capture), pedipalps (sensory), four pairs of walking legs. Mostly terrestrial in warm/dry habitats; no antennae. Medically important as vectors (ticks) and envenomers.

Class 5

Crustacea — Crabs, Krill, Woodlice

Three tagmata: head, thorax, abdomen. Two pairs of antennae; complicated mouthparts (two pairs of maxillae + one pair of mandibles). Exoskeleton reinforced with calcium carbonate (extra rigid). Mostly marine; some freshwater/terrestrial. Abdomen may have swimmerets.

Class 6

Insecta (Hexapoda)

Largest animal class; ~1 million described species. Three tagmata; one pair of antennae; complex, species-variable mouthparts; three pairs of legs; usually two pairs of wings (legs and wings attached to thorax). Compound eyes + simple eyes (ocelli). Tracheal respiration; Malpighian tubule excretion. Pheromones for communication. Can be hemimetabolous or holometabolous.

Metamorphosis Types Compared

Hemimetabolous (Incomplete)

Egg → Nymph → Adult
Nymph resembles adult but is sexually immature and usually wingless.
Example: cockroach, grasshopper

Holometabolous (Complete)

Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult
Larva looks and behaves completely differently from the adult (different morphology, diet, habitat).
Example: butterfly, mosquito, beetle

Understanding the Vectorial System
The Core Triad

The vectorial system is built on three interacting components that must overlap simultaneously and spatially for disease transmission to occur:

① Pathogen (The Agent)

The microorganism that causes disease:

  • Bacteria
  • Parasites
  • Viruses
② Vector (The Carrier)

Living organism that carries pathogen from host to host — often without getting sick:

  • Mosquitoes
  • Ticks
  • Sandflies
  • Biting midges
  • Fleas
③ Vertebrate Host (The Victim)

Organism that gets infected by the pathogen:

  • Humans
  • Animals
🔺
The orange triangle in the vectorial system diagram represents the zone of pathogen transmission — infection only occurs when all three elements (pathogen, vector, host) converge in the same time and place, within an environmental context.
Environmental Context

The outer circle of the system. The environment dictates whether vectors and pathogens can survive:

Forested environments
Natural habitats where ticks thrive; wild-animal reservoir hosts
Urban environments
Dense human populations; standing water; ideal for mosquitoes and fleas

Mosquitoes (Family Culicidae)

There are 3,539 species of mosquitoes globally, but only ~15% feed on humans. They are divided into two subfamilies:

Anophelinae — 540 species

Anopheles genus. Found worldwide except polar regions. Primary vectors of malaria (Plasmodium spp.). Resting position: body angled, proboscis aligned.

Culicinae — >3,000 species

Aedes and Culex genera. Found on every continent except Antarctica. Aedes transmits dengue, Zika, chikungunya, yellow fever. Culex transmits West Nile virus, filariasis.

Female Mosquito Blood-Feeding Mechanism

Only female mosquitoes take blood meals — they need the protein for egg maturation. During feeding, the proboscis penetrates the skin and the mosquito injects saliva containing three key anti-haemostatic compounds:

Apyrase
Inhibits platelet aggregation → keeps blood flowing freely at the bite site
Anti-thrombin factors
Prevent blood coagulation (clotting) → maintains liquid blood flow
Sialokinin
Counters vasoconstriction → dilates blood vessels for better access

Diseases transmitted by mosquito bite: Dengue, Yellow fever, West Nile virus, Zika, Rift Valley fever, Chikungunya

Virus in the Mosquito — Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP)
1
Ingestion

Mosquito feeds on an infected host. The virus (along with the blood meal) enters the midgut lumen.

2a
Midgut Replication

The virus infects midgut epithelial cells, replicates, crosses the basal lamina into the haemocoel (body cavity), and disseminates through haemolymph.

2b
Salivary Gland Invasion (EIP)

The virus invades and replicates in the salivary glands. The time from ingestion to salivary gland infection is the Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP). Duration is influenced by temperature, humidity, and virus strain.

3
Transmission

The now-infectious mosquito bites a healthy host. Virus is injected via saliva into the skin, initiating a new infection cycle.

Factors Affecting Pathogen Transmission
Internal Factors
  • Vector–pathogen co-evolution and genomic interactions
  • Vector immune responses (RNAi, Toll, IMD pathways)
  • Microbiota (bacteria, insect-specific viruses)
  • Co-infections by multiple pathogens
  • Molecular host–vector interactions
External Factors

Biotic:

  • Adult nutrition (nectar sources)
  • Host blood type and quality
  • Larval nutrition and breeding site quality

Abiotic:

  • Temperature — affects EIP and survival
  • Altitude and topography
  • Chemical environment (insecticides)

Lecture Three
Importance of the Skin at the Host–Vector Interface
Structure of the Skin

The skin is the body's largest organ, serving simultaneously as a physical barrier, a chemical barrier, and a complex immune organ. It consists of three primary layers:

Layer 1 — Epidermis

Outermost layer. Provides a waterproof barrier. Contains keratinocytes (primary immune cells of skin), melanocytes (pigment), Langerhans cells (dendritic cells), and DETC (dendritic epidermal T cells). Topped by the stratum corneum — dead, keratinised cells.

Layer 2 — Dermis

Middle layer of tough connective tissue. Contains hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands (oil), sensory nerve endings, fibroblasts, mast cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, neutrophils, and an extensive vascular and lymphatic network.

Layer 3 — Hypodermis

Deepest layer (subcutaneous tissue). Composed of fat cells (adipocytes), collagen, and fibroblasts. Functions: thermal insulation, mechanical cushioning, energy storage, anchoring skin to underlying muscle.

Immunity of the Skin

1. Innate Immunity — Keratinocytes as First Responders

Keratinocytes are far more than structural cells — they are active immune sentinels:

Keratinocyte Immune Signalling Pathway
1
Detection via Toll-like Receptors (TLRs)

TLRs on keratinocyte surfaces detect bacterial signals: TLR1/2 — lipoproteins/zymosan; TLR4 — LPS (gram-negative bacteria); TLR5 — flagellin. Endosomal TLR7 detects ssRNA (viruses); TLR3 detects dsRNA; TLR9 detects unmethylated CpG DNA.

2
NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation

Danger signals (UV light, toxins, irritants, LPS) trigger the NLRP3 inflammasome complex within the cytoplasm. NLRP3 + ASC activate Caspase-1, which cleaves Pro-IL-1β → mature IL-1β (potent pro-inflammatory cytokine).

3
Gene Activation & Effector Production

Signals reach the nucleus → DNA transcription produces: Antimicrobial peptides (natural antibiotics: defensins, cathelicidins); Cytokines (IL-1β, TNF, IL-6, IL-8) — coordinate systemic immune response; Chemokines (CXCL1, CCL2) — recruit neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages to the infection site.

2. Adaptive Immunity — Cells of the Dermis

The dermis houses a rich adaptive immune cell population:

  • Langerhans cells — epidermal dendritic cells; sample antigens and migrate to lymph nodes
  • Dermal dendritic cells (DC) — present antigens to naive T cells; produce pro-inflammatory cytokines
  • CD8⁺ T cells — cytotoxic; kill virus-infected cells directly
  • CD4⁺ T cells — helper; orchestrate immune response via cytokines
  • γδ T cells — rapid-response T cells active at mucosal/skin barriers
  • Innate lymphoid cells (ILC) — cytokine producers without antigen specificity
  • Mast cells — release histamine, tryptase, chymase in response to arthropod saliva
  • Neutrophils & Macrophages — phagocytose pathogens; produce reactive oxygen species

3. Skin Microbiota — The Microbial Barrier

The Human Microbiome Project (Grice & Segre, 2011)

The skin is a 2 m² ecosystem harbouring over 10¹² microorganisms. Key residents include:

Bacteria:
  • Staphylococcus epidermidis
  • Cutibacterium acnes
  • Corynebacterium striatum
  • Streptococcus spp.
Fungi, Mites, Viruses:
  • Malassezia (fungus)
  • Demodex (mite)
  • Staphylococcus phage
  • Human papillomavirus / polyomavirus
🦟
Skin microbiota and mosquito attractiveness: Brevibacterium epidermidis on the feet produces volatile compounds that attract Anopheles gambiae (malaria vector). This mirrors the fact that Limburger cheese (which contains the same bacterium) attracts mosquitoes in laboratory experiments. (Knols & de Jong, 1996)
Skin as the Site of Pathogen Inoculation

When an arthropod bites, it injects both pathogens and saliva into the skin. Saliva contains immunomodulatory compounds that suppress the host immune response, facilitating pathogen establishment.

Lyme Borreliosis — Detailed Progression

The skin is the entry point, multiplication site, and reservoir of Borrelia bacteria:

1
Tick Attachment (Long Blood Meal)

Tick attaches for hours to days; during feeding, Borrelia migrates from tick midgut → salivary glands → host skin. Causes local injury and potential necrosis at bite site.

2
Early Local Stage — Erythema Migrans

Bacteria multiply in skin → expanding bull's-eye rash (erythema migrans); local immune inflammation. Hallmark sign of Lyme disease.

3
Early Disseminating Stage — Neuroborreliosis

Bacteria enter bloodstream → spread to nervous system → facial nerve palsy, meningitis, radicular pain (Neuroborreliosis).

4
Late Stage — ACA & Arthritis

Acrodermatitis chronica atrophicans (ACA): chronic skin inflammation and thinning on extremities. Lyme arthritis: bacteria in joints → severe swelling, especially knees.

Arboviruses — Role of Mosquito Saliva (Aedes aegypti)

Mosquito saliva is not merely a vehicle for pathogens — it is an active virulence factor:

  • Salivary serine proteases increase endothelial permeability → oedema → viral retention in skin
  • Saliva-induced IL-4↑, IL-10↑, IL-12↑ but IFN-γ↓, TNF-α↓, IFN-β↑ → immune suppression favours viral replication
  • Salivary proteins activate myeloid cells (macrophages, Langerhans cells) → these become viral targets and carriers to lymph nodes
  • IgE and IgG sensitisation from repeated mosquito bites alters immune responses in subsequent bites
  • CCL2/4/5/7/12 upregulation → recruits more permissive host cells to bite site

Lecture Four
RNAi-Mediated Antiviral Immunity in Insects
Why Are Insects Efficient Vectors?

The key to understanding vector efficiency lies in the nature of viral infection in insects compared to vertebrates. In vertebrates, viruses typically cause acute, life-threatening disease. In insects, a remarkable balance is established:

Acute Infection (vertebrate pattern)

Viral load spikes rapidly and dramatically, then drops sharply. The host either clears the virus (immune success) or dies. Transmission window is very short.

Persistent Infection (insect pattern)

Viral load rises slowly to a stable, controlled low level and remains there for the insect's entire life. The insect stays healthy and infectious indefinitely. Transmission potential is lifelong.

⚖️
The Balance Model: Viral persistence results from an equilibrium between viral replication (pushing viral load up) and immune mechanisms (pushing viral load down). This balance is maintained by two key immune systems: RNAi and viral DNA (vDNA) production.

Because the insect does not die from infection, it becomes a permanent, living reservoir — flying around and transmitting viruses like dengue, Zika, and chikungunya to humans with every subsequent blood meal.

RNAi — The Primary Antiviral Mechanism

RNA interference (RNAi) is the dominant antiviral defence pathway in insects. It is triggered by the presence of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which is a by-product of RNA virus replication (not normally found in healthy cells).

The siRNA (Small Interfering RNA) Pathway — Step by Step
1
Viral Replication Produces dsRNA

An RNA virus enters the cell and begins replicating its genome. During this process, a complementary strand is synthesised, producing viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA).

2
Dicer-2 Cleavage

The enzyme Dicer-2 (an RNase III family enzyme) recognises and cleaves the viral dsRNA into short fragments of exactly 21 nucleotides called small interfering RNAs (siRNAs).

3
RISC Assembly (RNA-Induced Silencing Complex)

The siRNA duplex is loaded into the RISC complex. The protein Argonaute-2 (AGO2) is the catalytic core of RISC. The "passenger" strand of the siRNA is discarded; the "guide" strand remains.

4
Viral RNA Degradation

The guide strand directs RISC to complementary viral RNA sequences. AGO2's "slicer" activity cleaves the viral RNA, degrading it and preventing further protein synthesis. The RISC complex is then recycled.

⚠️
Critical limitation: RNAi alone is not sufficient to achieve viral persistence. Experimental disruption of RNAi (using mutant mosquitoes lacking Dicer-2) results in lethal viral infections — the insect dies rather than establishing a balanced, persistent infection. A second complementary mechanism is therefore required.
Viral DNA (vDNA) Production During RNA Virus Infection

The second key mechanism of antiviral immunity in insects is the conversion of viral RNA into viral DNA (vDNA) — a remarkable finding because RNA viruses do not normally produce DNA.

How vDNA is Produced — The Retrotransposon Mechanism
1
Retrotransposon Activation

Retrotransposons are mobile genetic elements in the insect genome that encode their own reverse transcriptase (an enzyme that converts RNA → DNA). Viral RNA infection activates these elements.

2
Reverse Transcription of Viral RNA

Retrotransposon reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). This cDNA is then incorporated into a circular viral DNA molecule.

3
vDNA as a Persistent Template

The vDNA is stable and persists in the cell. It is transcribed into vDNA-derived RNA transcripts (both sense and antisense strands).

4
Continuous siRNA Production

The double-stranded transcripts from vDNA are processed by Dicer-2 into vDNA-derived siRNAs. These continuously replenish the RISC pool, sustaining antiviral silencing even when active viral replication subsides — acting as an immune memory.

🧬
Combined model: RNAi + vDNA together balance viral replication to achieve stable, persistent, low-level infection. Neither mechanism alone is sufficient. vDNA provides long-term "memory" that maintains the RNAi response even between blood meals.
Evidence — vDNA Found Across Many Invertebrates

The vDNA mechanism has been documented for multiple viruses across different arthropod hosts, confirming it is a conserved evolutionary strategy:

  • Drosophila C virus
  • Flock House virus
  • Sindbis virus
Mosquito (Aedes/Culex)
  • West Nile virus
  • La Crosse virus

  • Shrimp parvovirus

Proof of Concept — The AZT Experiment
Targeting Retrotransposons with AZT (Lambrechts & Saleh, 2019; Goic et al., 2016)

AZT (Azidothymidine / Zidovudine) is an antiretroviral drug that inhibits reverse transcriptase, blocking retrotransposon activity and thus preventing vDNA production.

Experimental design: Mosquitoes were pre-treated with AZT for 1 day, then given an infectious blood meal containing Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), AZT alone, or CHIKV+AZT.

Survival Results:
  • AZT only: ~100% survival (drug non-toxic)
  • CHIKV only: ~75% survival (persistent infection)
  • CHIKV + AZT: ~0% survival — mosquitoes died
